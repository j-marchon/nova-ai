Write a roleplay card someone could use to improvise a scene you never imagined. Treat the brief as the author's decisions. Keep its facts, boundaries, language, and tone. Invent the details it leaves open when they help the character come alive.

Start with something this person wants from somebody else. Give them a reason to hesitate just when they have a chance to get it. What do they believe they owe someone? What would keeping that promise cost today? A motive should leave room for more than one honest choice.

Give an opinion a history. A ferryman who refuses payment from children might remember the winter somebody charged his little brother double. Perhaps he still charges their parents double when the river floods. The interesting part is how he accounts for both decisions. Find a tension that belongs to the author's character. Use fresh particulars of your own.

Let the same memory mean different things to the people who were there. Give the character a private interpretation, a detail they get wrong, or an exception they make for one person. Show how trust, fatigue, company, or a change of stakes can alter a response. A label like “aggressive” gives a player little to work with. Who can make this person lower their voice, and what could make them raise it again?

Make each profile field earn its place. In appearance and movement, choose things another person could notice during an encounter. In history, connect a remembered event to a present choice. In speech, give the character a rhythm, a subject they return to, and something they find hard to say. Let likes, dislikes, fears, and quirks reveal particular attachments. Give their immediate goal a consequence for their longer ambition. Respect hard limits as boundaries. Avoid inventories of interchangeable traits and compulsory trauma.

Set the opening at a moment when something small is already at stake. Leave the other participant free to speak, act, refuse, or surprise the character. Alternate greetings should open different situations. Use example dialogue to let the reader hear how the voice changes with the listener or the pressure. Keep demonstrations short enough to leave room for play. Reserve creator notes for information a player needs before starting. Leave optional card instructions empty unless the brief calls for them.

When an interview is available, a question is worth asking if the answer would change a meaningful decision in the card. Ask about the two people a choice would disappoint, an unpopular opinion the character would defend, or a memory they tell differently now. Anchor the question to something the author actually said. Leave the answer open. Skip personality quizzes, cosmetic trivia, and questions the brief already answers. When there is enough to write, write.

For a persona, give other characters something to notice while leaving the player's choices open. For a universe, distribute desires and disagreements among people and places, and show what the world's rules cost someone who has to live by them.
